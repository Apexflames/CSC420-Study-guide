Topic 1

Concept of Information Systems

Systems, DIKW, the components of an IS, and the organizational pyramid of computer-based information systems.

What Is a System?

Source
A system is a group of interrelated components working together toward a common goal by accepting inputs and producing outputs in an organized transformation process.
Plain English"System" comes from the Greek systema — an organised relationship among the parts of something. Any system — a car, a company, a computer — is just parts working together toward one goal.
  • Structure of a system: Input (people, raw materials, money) → Processing (transformation using energy, procedures, employees, machines) → Output (products, served customers).
  • Closed system — totally independent of and isolated from its environment. Open system — significantly interconnected with its environment, exchanging information, material, energy, people.
  • Systems approach — a set of procedures/scientific methods for solving a problem, caring about the inter-relatedness of elements. Systems analysis — a management technique for designing a new system or improving an existing one.

Feedback and Control

Plain EnglishA system with feedback and control is a cybernetic system — it monitors and regulates itself. Feedback is data about the system's performance (e.g. sales performance data to a sales manager). Control is using that feedback to decide whether the system is on track and adjust its inputs/processing if not.

Characteristics of a System (as supplied)

  • Organisation — structure and order; the arrangement of components that helps achieve objectives (e.g. a hierarchical org chart; a computer's input device, CPU, output device and storage units linked together).
  • Interaction — how each component functions with other components (e.g. purchasing interacts with production; the CPU interacts with memory and other units).
  • Interdependence — components depend on one another, coordinated and linked to achieve an objective.
  • Integration — how the system is tied together; parts work together even though each performs a unique function, producing a better result as a whole.
  • Central Objective — the objective (real or stated) that all users must be aware of in advance.

Types of Systems

ClassificationMeaning
Physical or AbstractPhysical = tangible, static or dynamic. Abstract = conceptual/non-physical (e.g. formulas, models)
Open or ClosedOpen = continually interacts with its environment (e.g. an information system). Closed = isolated from environmental influences (rare in reality)
Deterministic or ProbabilisticDeterministic = next state perfectly predictable (e.g. a numerically controlled machine tool). Probabilistic = occurrence of events cannot be perfectly predicted (e.g. a warehouse and its contents)
Man-made Information SystemsA set of devices, procedures and operating systems designed around user-based criteria to produce and communicate information for planning, control and performance

Formal, Informal & Computer-based Information Systems

  • Formal Information System — based on the organization chart; a map of positions and authority relationships, concerned with authority, communication and work flow.
  • Informal Information System — an employee-based system meeting personal/vocational needs, helping solve work-related problems and funnelling information upward through indirect channels.
  • Computer-based Information Systems (CBIS) — the third category; depends mainly on the computer for handling business applications.

Why Information Systems Are Vital Today (5 functions, as supplied)

  • Enhanced Decision-Making — accurate, real-time data reduces reliance on "gut feelings."
  • Coordination & Collaboration — teams across the globe work on the same data via cloud-based platforms.
  • Control — managers monitor performance against goals and spot anomalies immediately.
  • Analysis & Visualization — complex data becomes charts/graphs/heatmaps, revealing trends.
  • Competitive Advantage — an efficient IS lowers costs, improves service, and enables new business models (e.g. Netflix, Uber).

Likely Question

Q: Define a system, and explain the three basic interacting components of a dynamic system.

A system is a group of interrelated components working together toward a common goal by accepting inputs and producing outputs in an organized transformation process. Its three basic components are: (1) Input — capturing and assembling elements to be processed (raw materials, energy, data, human effort); (2) Processing — the transformation process that converts input into output (e.g. manufacturing, calculations); (3) Output — transferring the processed elements to their ultimate destination (finished products, services, information).

Likely Question

Q: Differentiate between a Transaction Processing System, a Management Information System, and a Decision Support System.

A TPS is the most fundamental computer-based system — it captures, classifies, stores and retrieves transaction data (e.g. billing, hiring, placing orders) for routine, operational-level work. An MIS enlarges the scope of data processing to support management and administrative activities — planning, scheduling, analysis and decision-making — for tactical-level managers. A DSS assists managers facing unstructured or semi-structured decisions that are not recurring and for which no clear procedure exists; it supports, but does not replace, managerial judgement.

Topic 2

Informatics

The study of information itself — its structure, behaviour and interactions — and how it differs from Information Systems.

Source
Informatics is an interdisciplinary field that focuses on the structure, behavior, and interactions of natural and artificial systems that store, process, and communicate information.

Informatics vs. Information Systems

InformaticsInformation Systems
FocusTheoretical underpinnings and fundamental nature of information — data representation, information theory, philosophical implicationsApplied — design, development, implementation and management of systems supporting organizational processes and decisions
AsksHow does information work, and what are its inherent properties?How can information technology be deployed to achieve business/organizational goals?
Plain EnglishInformatics is the theory; Information Systems is the practical application of that theory inside organizations.

Informatics in the Context of Information Systems

Informatics explores the Socio-technical Triad — the relationship between Information, Technology, and People — through:

  • Human-Computer Interaction (HCI) — aligning digital-system design with human cognitive capabilities and limitations.
  • Information Architecture — structuring complex data environments so information is discoverable and logically organized.
  • Social Informatics — analyzing how a new information system changes an organization's culture, power structures and workflows.

Likely Question

Q: Distinguish between Informatics and Information Systems, and explain how they relate to each other.

Informatics focuses on the theoretical underpinnings of information itself — how it is represented, structured and governed by information theory — asking how information works and what its properties are. Information Systems is more applied: it concerns the design, development, implementation and management of systems that use information technology to support organizational processes and decision-making. They relate because Informatics provides the theoretical framework that Information Systems applies to build practical, organization-specific solutions.

Likely Question

Q: Name and briefly describe the four historical eras in the evolution of informatics.

(1) The Pre-Digital/Mechanical Era (pre-1940s) — physical information processing via the abacus, Pascal's calculator, and punch cards. (2) The Birth of Foundation Theory (1940s–50s) — Claude Shannon's Information Theory (1948) and the first electronic computers like ENIAC. (3) Institutional & Mainframe Growth (1960s–80s) — the term "informatics" was coined, and Edgar Codd invented the Relational Database Management System (1970) to organize mainframe data. (4) The Network & AI Revolution (1990s–present) — the World Wide Web, Big Data, and Cloud Computing.

Topic 3

Organisation and Methods (O&M)

A management service for diagnosing inefficiency and improving how an organization works — the SREDIM methodology.

Source
Continuous checking of office work at every stage and continuous research for the improvement and simplification of office methods is collectively known as Organisation and Methods or O & M study.
Plain EnglishThink of O&M as a clinical check-up for a business: it diagnoses inefficiencies, bottlenecks and redundancies, then prescribes streamlined workflows.

O&M developed during the Second World War to improve government office administration, later adopted by big business and even small concerns. An independent department (not involved in routine office work) may be set up, or outside Management Consultants engaged.

Two Core Components (as supplied)

AspectFocuses onExamples
"Organization"Structural framework of the enterpriseSpan of control; delegation & authority; departmentalization
"Methods"Mechanics of day-to-day workProcess mapping; form design; office automation

Objectives of an O&M Unit

  • Eliminate Waste — reduce wasted time, material, effort and space.
  • Standardize Operations — uniform procedures performed predictably and correctly every time.
  • Improve Quality — optimized workflows reduce human error.
  • Enhance Employee Morale — clearing ambiguous roles and redundant paperwork reduces frustration.

Key O&M Tools and Techniques

  • Work Flowcharts — visual diagrams tracking the movement of a document, product or decision through its stages.
  • Work Measurement (Time & Motion Studies) — timing tasks to establish realistic performance standards and identify delays.
  • Work Distribution Charts — a matrix showing what tasks are done in a department, by whom, and how much time each takes, highlighting unfair/inefficient workload distribution.

Five Aspects of an O&M Assignment (as supplied)

  1. Determining the Purpose — the object of the assignment must be clearly known and written down before a plan of action is chalked out.
  2. Planning the Assignment — procedure and extent of study depend on department size and area of operation.
  3. Collecting the Data — e.g. via questionnaire forms; data must be relevant or time, money and energy are wasted.
  4. Making Analysis of the Procedure — querying every aspect: purpose, method, forms/documents used, time taken, and who does it.
  5. Making Proposals — the best of several alternative methods is submitted as a recommendation for action; may suggest additional staff or retrenchment. Success depends on staff cooperation — the O&M team must not be seen as a "fault-finding commission."

Why O&M Matters Today

Modern concepts like Business Process Reengineering (BPR), Lean Management, and Agile operations are the evolution of O&M's same core philosophy — whether cutting paperwork in a government ministry or automating an AI-driven workflow in a tech startup.

Advantages & Disadvantages of O&M (as supplied)

Click to expand — full list

Advantages:

  • Improves methods of operation, the organisation as a whole, and its administration.
  • Work simplification, better communication, more effective control, prevention/reduction of faulty steps.
  • Introduction of new techniques and equipment, raising productivity.
  • General improvement of staff through more careful selection and training.
  • Work measurement and job evaluation become practicable (affecting remuneration, promotion).
  • Greater security — wastage, fraud etc. are detected and prevented.
  • Cost reduction and enhanced organizational prestige.

Disadvantages:

  • It is itself a costly affair.
  • Small organisations cannot afford a separate O&M department (though contract experts can be engaged).
  • Difficult to apply properly — too much work simplification is bad.

Likely Question

Q: Explain the SREDIM methodology used in Organisation and Methods studies.

SREDIM is a six-step, data-driven approach: Select the specific area/process needing improvement (usually one with high costs, delays or errors); Record all facts about current operations using interviews, observations and flowcharts; Examine the current method critically, questioning its necessity, who performs it, and whether it can be faster/cheaper; Develop a new, improved structure or workflow; Install the approved solution, including staff training and updated manuals; and Maintain the new system through periodic review so staff don't revert to old habits.

Likely Question

Q: Discuss the advantages and disadvantages of Organisation and Methods (O&M) studies.

Advantages include improved methods of operation and administration, work simplification, better communication, more effective control, introduction of new techniques/equipment, staff improvement through better selection/training, practicable work measurement and job evaluation, greater security against wastage/fraud, and overall cost reduction. Disadvantages include the cost of running an O&M exercise itself, the inability of small organisations to maintain a dedicated O&M department (though they can hire outside consultants), and the difficulty of applying O&M correctly — excessive work simplification can itself become counterproductive.
